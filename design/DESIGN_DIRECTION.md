# Design Direction

## 0) Current State Snapshot (updated for the repo)

The current site already does several things right:
- It has a clear message: AI and automation are used to reduce operational friction, not to add shiny features.
- The written positioning is specific and credible: workflow bottlenecks, ownership boundaries, proof-of-value, and human checkpoints.
- The homepage, case-study summaries, and article framing all align around a practical builder identity rather than a generic freelance portfolio.
- The site already communicates strategic thinking and implementation capability better than most personal tech brands.

The main opportunities are not content invention but design sharpening:
- The first screen still feels more like an accomplished business landing page than a recognisable technical brand.
- The current composition is solid, but not yet memorable enough to survive the "generic portfolio" test.
- The concept of a system/decision motif is present in the content, but it has not yet been fully translated into a consistent, observable visual grammar.
- Proof is good in written form; it needs to be stronger visually so visitors can inspect the system rather than just read about it.

### Best material to preserve
- The 5-step prototype method.
- The ownership boundary language.
- The "where AI is not the right tool" stance.
- The practical emphasis on measurable outcomes and operational constraints.
- The strong, non-hype positioning around real workflow pain.

## 1) Why The Current Direction Still Feels Safe

### What is already strong
- Positioning is specific: decision-heavy workflows, boundaries, evidence, and implementation.
- Content avoids hype and already has useful tension: "do not use AI where deterministic automation wins."
- Technical constraints are realistic for Astro and performance.
- The site reads as a serious practitioner, not a template-driven freelancer.

### What still feels template-like
- The page rhythm is too even. Every section enters with the same visual pattern and weight.
- The first screen still reads as a "hero plus support graphic" pattern that appears across modern portfolio templates.
- The motif is present but passive. It guides the eye, but it does not actively demonstrate reasoning.
- Proof is still mostly read, not inspected.
- The visual system is stronger in content than in graphic identity.

## 2) Explored Directions

## Direction A: Protocol Theatre (Rejected)

### Concept
The homepage opens as a command protocol sheet with large event markers and staged transitions.

### Why rejected
- Risk of performative "futurism UI".
- Too theatrical for long-form credibility.
- Would age quickly and invite style over substance.

## Direction B: Orchestration Ledger HQ (Chosen)

### Concept
Treat the site as a living decision ledger: each section is an entry in an engineering operating record, not a marketing section.

### Emotional impression
Authored, technical, decisive, slightly contrarian, and grounded.

### Why this is distinct
- The core motif is structural, not decorative: a persistent ledger spine with fork tabs and boundary stamps.
- Every major block answers one systems question: signal, ownership, mechanism, or proof.
- The homepage first viewport is a "Decision Review Board" composition, not a standard hero.

## Direction C: Independent Lab Notebook (Rejected)

### Concept
Highly editorial, writing-first experience inspired by research notebooks.

### Why rejected
- Excellent for writing depth but weaker for proving build capability quickly.
- Under-serves founders and CTOs who need immediate implementation evidence.

## 3) Chosen Concept: Orchestration Ledger HQ

## Central design idea
The website behaves like the control surface of Dave's engineering judgment. Visitors do not just read claims; they inspect decisions.

## Signature visual motif
Orchestration Ledger:
- A persistent vertical spine that represents workflow continuity.
- Fork tabs that enter content blocks to mark decisions or exceptions.
- Boundary stamps that identify where ownership changes.
- Evidence seals that mark measured outcomes.

The motif is semantic UI grammar. If a motif element appears, it must map to real meaning.

## Slightly risky decision (intentional)
The first viewport deprioritizes conventional self-introduction.
- The main visual object is a Decision Review Board table with four rows:
	1. Signal
	2. Decision owner
	3. Automation route
	4. Proof gate
- Dave's name and role appear as a confident but smaller context marker.

Why this risk is justified:
- It immediately communicates point of view through structure.
- It filters for the right audience: people who value reasoning and implementation.

## Uncommon interaction (non-template)
Global Failure Mode Lens toggle.
- A compact toggle in nav: "Failure Lens: Off/On".
- When enabled, every diagram, project card, and process block overlays exception paths, human override points, and known failure modes.
- This turns the site itself into evidence of systems thinking and risk ownership.

Why this matters:
- Most portfolios show success paths only.
- Showing failure paths is a credibility signal to CTO-level visitors.
- It aligns well with the current content strategy: the site already talks about ownership boundaries and constraint-aware decisions.

## Recommended next-step refinements

The current site already has the right content and positioning, so the next refinements should focus on form rather than rewriting the narrative.

1. Strengthen the homepage first viewport into an editorial Decision Review Board.
   - Replace the generic photo-led hero with a diagrammatic or annotated board.
   - Use four rows of system logic instead of a standard headline + image arrangement.
   - Let the first view show how Dave thinks about risk, ownership, and proof.

2. Make the signature motif visible across the entire site.
   - Reuse ledger lines, fork markers, decision traces, and evidence stamps across project pages and article layouts.
   - Do not make the motif decorative; every element should correspond to a real engineering concept.

3. Turn proof into inspectable diagrams.
   - Use one small architecture diagram or process flow for each featured project.
   - Show before/after workflow paths, decision ownership, exception routes, and measured outcomes.

4. Add a restrained “failure lens” enhancement as progressive enhancement.
   - Keep the default experience readable and strong without JS.
   - Use optional toggles to reveal risk paths and override points for deeper reading.

5. Tighten the visual hierarchy between thesis, evidence, and implementation.
   - The page should feel like a system review rather than a marketing page.
   - Each section should clearly answer a specific question: what was the problem, who owned the decision, how did the system work, what changed, how was it proven?

## Audience fit
- CTOs and heads of engineering: quickly assess boundary rigor and operational realism.
- Technical founders: see rapid prototyping and decision velocity.
- Engineering leaders: recognize practical SDLC latency reduction patterns.
- Business leaders: understand value without needing deep AI jargon.

## Influences (for authorial feel, not imitation)
- Builder-owned personal sites with clear point of view and strong editorial control.
- Architecture review artifacts.
- Decision logs and incident timeline notation.

## Explicitly avoid
- Gradient blobs, glass cards, logo walls, and interchangeable SaaS cards.
- Decorative terminal windows or fake dashboards.
- Oversized hero + two button default pattern.
- Generic portfolio photos that do not support the narrative.
- Any motif use that is visually clever but semantically empty.
- “AI hype” phrasing that competes with the site’s otherwise grounded positioning.

## 4) Anti-Template Critique

## Could this be swapped to another engineer with only a name change?
Not if implemented correctly, because the visual system depends on:
- ownership boundaries,
- failure-path visibility,
- proof-first case structure,
- and recurring orchestration ledger grammar.

If the implementation drifts back to generic card stacks, reject and iterate.

## Does first screen have a memorable idea?
Yes: the Decision Review Board as the primary opening composition.

## Does it show build capability, not just opinion?
Yes: proof sections are tied to system boundaries, exception handling, and outcomes.

## Usability risk check
The interaction model is progressive enhancement. Without JavaScript, all failure-path information remains visible in plain text and static diagrams.
